## 1 af_heart
Every company has rules.

How much vacation you get. What a customer dinner may cost. Who approves a trip.

And every day, people ask for things.

...

TrustDesk is a workspace where those requests meet your rules. An AI reads your own policy documents, and gives advice.

But the advice is only advice. A person always makes the decision.

## 2 af_heart
First start. There are no key files to copy, and no default password.

The server prints a one-time code in its log.

Whoever holds that code creates the administrator password, right here in the browser.

That is a deliberate choice. Until someone completes this step, every page of the application refuses to answer.

...

The password needs at least twelve characters.

Now we are in. And the workspace is empty.

## 3 am_michael
Now, some data to work with. We load a fictional company called Acme.

One click adds sixteen policy documents, eleven employees, and a mix of good and problematic requests.

[pause 3.0]

Here they are. Each one was sent to the AI, in the background.

For each one, the system finds the policy passages that fit, and asks the model for a first opinion.

That is all the model does. It reads, and it advises. It never decides.

Look at the advice column.

[pause 1.5]

Every request now has a first opinion.

...

The policies live here. Markdown, text, or PDF.

Open one, and you see the passages it was split into.

They are stored in Postgres, with one index for meaning, and one for exact words. Both live in the same database as everything else.

The model that does the checking is chosen in Settings.

It sees the request text and the policy passages. Names, e-mail addresses, and employee numbers never leave.

## 4 af_sarah
Now the AI advice. Open the request called Lunch with auditor, again.

This is the review window. The advice is on the right.

Before the model says anything, the system computes facts by itself. Working days, notice given, overlaps, and possible duplicates.

A model is poor at counting days. So we do not ask it to.

Here, the system noticed that this lunch is identical to another one.

...

Below that, the reasons. Each reason points to a passage of your policy, with a quotation.

TrustDesk checks that the quotation really appears in the passage, word for word.

If it does not, the quote is marked as unverified.

And a yes or a no with no rule behind it is downgraded to needs more information.

On the left, the history. Who did what, and when.

The advice is a first reading. It saves the approver the search for the rule. It does not replace the judgment.

## 5 af_sarah
Now the human part. This request has advice to reject.

The approver disagrees. Watch what happens if they simply press Approve.

[pause 1.5]

TrustDesk asks for a written reason. At least ten characters, because going against the advice has to be explained.

So the approver writes one, and approves again.

[pause 1.5]

The decision is recorded under the approver's own name, together with what the AI had recommended at that moment.

Rejecting a request, or asking for more information, needs a note too. The employee will read it.

Nothing is hidden. The decision, its reason, and the advice it overruled stay together.

## 6 am_eric
Now, the reports. Managers and auditors want the overall picture.

So there are nine reports. Volumes and outcomes. Expenses by department. Who is away next month.

...

This one matters most. Every decision that went against the advice, with its justification.

Any report can be printed, or downloaded as a spreadsheet. Let's download this one.

Done.

## 7 am_eric
Where do requests come from? From your own applications.

Say, an HR portal. It starts by creating an employee, through a small web API.

[pause 1.5]

Then it sends a request. A key in the call makes a repeat safe, so there are no duplicates.

[pause 2.0]

Back in the app, the request is already in the queue.

Now one last call, to read it back.

[pause 1.5]

The application sees the status. But the advice field is not there.

The token that sends requests can never read the advice, or decide. Only people can.

## 8 af_heart
Now, searching the rules. The documents can be searched in plain language.

[pause 1.5]

Every result shows where it came from. The document, the heading, and the version.

Assistants such as Claude Code or Cursor can search them too, through a read-only MCP connection.

MCP is a standard way for an assistant to use a tool.

The Connect tab has the setup, with this server's address already filled in.

## 9 af_heart
To recap. Load your rules. Let your applications send requests.

TrustDesk adds facts, finds the rules, and offers advice with quoted evidence.

A person decides. Every override is explained, and reported.

Everything lives in one Postgres database.
